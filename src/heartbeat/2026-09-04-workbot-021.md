Both tournament blockers closed. The fifth booth shipped, got played, and was parked as finished; NPCs started using the casino, which turned out to be what three other venues were waiting for; and a nine-item polish pass produced the clearest lesson of the month about how bug reports work.

## TC5: mirror trading

The fifth booth had been a lending market on paper, and the verdict on that design was *"this is all still too complicated for the game."* What replaced it is simpler and better: **you invest in a person.**

One booth, one scrollable list of every trader in P&L order — the leaderboard, which players already read — each a landscape row of that trader's stats. Put money up as a mirror trader and it moves **proportionally to their performance**, up when they do well, down when they lose, withdrawable whenever. Being copied pays: 5% of what they made as trading cash, 1% as commission. Margin-called players appear in the same list and can be offered terms to buy back in.

**The capacity cap is the design, and the choice of denominator is the whole argument.** A stake pays `capital × pnl / base`, so capping capital at a multiple of `base` bounds the house's exposure at that multiple of *what the trader actually made*. The two other candidates — position notional, or the trade-ladder rung — both leave `base` free to be tiny, so the bound **collapses**: a near-broke trader having one huge day prints money for whoever spotted them. Poisoning the cap to a flat figure in the test reports **300× their P&L against a 3× cap**.

It's set at 3× day-start equity. Day-start rather than live, so a row's capacity holds still for the day and rides the goal curve for free.

And **the cap isn't a patch, it's the booth's core tension**: good traders *fill up*, so you either pay to get in early or hunt someone the room hasn't noticed yet. That's the reading game, and it falls out of one number.

Three other decisions worth recording:

- **A margin call wipes mirror investors to zero.** Backing someone who blows up costs you everything behind them. It's the drama, and it keeps this an investment rather than a one-way bet.
- **The index is trading P&L only** — which wasn't in the design and turned out to be a correctness requirement. Any equity-derived index lets A-backs-B-backs-A feed itself, and it kills chain leverage before it exists.
- **Withdrawal needed no extra rule.** Instant and free, with capacity checked on entry so re-entry can be refused. Pulling out before a blow-up is a skill expression, not an exploit — you can only dodge *future* loss, never one the index has already taken.

Why it's better than what it replaced, in two lines: **it replaces a negotiation with a position** (the old comeback market priced a bespoke contract on three axes — 810 combinations, sealed, with a counterparty who could reject — where this asks how much, and in whom), and **it has customers**. The old one was bust-gated, and busts measured at zero on every day of fourteen. Everyone on the floor is investable all the time.

The loan manager and the old fund product were retired **by unbinding, not deletion** — they still exist and still work, they're simply unreachable from the floor. Cheap, reversible, and it can't break something that quietly depended on them.

### The layered ledger, and two rejected devices

Playing it produced a follow-up — *"a sparkline per player entry, and a bigger better formatted text explaining your current return. Keep it looking financial and professional."* Built as a trajectory column and a two-line return readout. Two things were built and thrown away in the process:

- **A shared y-domain across rows.** It makes rows directly comparable, and then one runaway trader at 8× flattens the other twenty-three into dead lines — **the rows that most need reading lose their shape to the one that needs it least.** Exact magnitudes sit two columns to the left anyway, so each cell autoscales.
- **The full-session x-axis.** It's the right device on the HUD's day track and the EOD ladder, where holding the whole day open makes the space to the right read as time remaining. Rendered into a 150px cell at bar 20 of 480, every trace was a **6px smudge against 144px of empty rule** — and the rule then read as an unfilled progress bar. *"How much day is left" is one global fact; a per-row cell shouldn't repeat it twenty-four times.*

Played and signed off — *"really clear to explain to people what you're doing"* — and parked as finished pending player feedback and the NPC AI pass.

## NPCs use the casino now

*"If possible I don't mind adding support for NPCs to take positions on all the other TCs even if it's random, so long as the mechanics behind that can be reused with the improved AI later."*

**The deliverable was the seam, not the cleverness.** Each decision is one pure function — a snapshot and a random source in, an intent out. No game-manager reference, no scene lookups, no state. The AI pass replaces functions, not plumbing.

It mattered more than "the room looks busier", because **three venues were structurally dead in single player**:

- **the block desk** — no counterparty at all; only the debug menu could put a quote on it
- **the fire sale** — a margin-called player received *nothing*, so the sealed auction never happened
- **mirror trading** — capacity never bound, so the booth's core tension was simply absent

The other three are solo bets against the house, where an NPC playing changes nothing the player can *do*. That split is worth keeping if this is ever trimmed.

Three traps, each of which shipped once and had to be measured out:

1. **The fire sale can't be on the think timer.** The auction window is 10 seconds; the think cadence is 12–34. Measured: **zero bids across a full day.** It's event-driven now, seeded when a sale opens.
2. **A fire-sale bid is not a stake.** Its basis is somebody's *whole position*. Measured on a live day-1 floor: **cheapest rung $600 against bot budgets of $53–$89.**
3. **The arrival distance is the booth's own reach, not a constant.** At a fixed 2.5m a bot walks into the cluster's collider and times out — so **bots walked to booths and then stood outside them doing nothing**, while the walk itself looked perfect.

Plus a nice cousin of an old bug: **mirror trading is the only booth with no stake ladder**, so nothing rounded a bot's intent up, and one was measured backing a trader with **$1**.

The measured cost is real and was shipped deliberately: the house edge takes 8–22% of floor equity across paired same-seed days. But margin calls and busts both stay at **zero**, so the reference floor's headline property survives.

**And a correction worth more than the feature.** Reading those numbers, two of Predator's floor markets looked broken. Both readings were wrong: one market's uncertainty band had **moved, not shrunk** (still two of six values carrying it, just further down the range), and the other going near-certain NO is *the design working* — it's a hook on a human having a monster day, so a floor of day-one retail bots **should** resolve it no. Which generalises: **a bot-only probe is a weak proxy for every "did something impressive happen" market.** Those metrics average over the whole floor, and at the tournament humans are 24 of the population. NPCs have never margin called, never cleared a goal on one trade, never had a big day — so the bot baseline calibrates only the boring half.

## Nine polish items, and the lesson underneath them

A cosmetic work order: nametags, per-day music, ticker duplicates, chart zoom, booth ring text, a floating P&L popup, the wall data panel, the shop menu boards, the day-transition sun, and a menu style pass. All nine done — but the valuable part is that **in five cases the reported symptom named the wrong thing**:

| reported | actual |
|---|---|
| "the sponsor rings don't have great word wrapping" | the draw call's width argument **clips, it does not wrap** — there was no wrapping at all, anywhere on those 50 faces |
| "the floor gets darker to pitch black then the sun springs to 9am" | **three** faults: the close was a cut not a fade, the sun swept yaw only so it never rose or set, and the dawn rewound 150° eastward |
| "the effect of the sun setting is lost by 2:30pm" | shadow **length** — the thing that says *late* — never changed, because elevation was constant all day |
| "menus could be more polished, consistent sizes, consistent audio" | **seven** button sizes, 111 ad-hoc theme overrides, and **zero** audio outside one screen |
| "ticker sometimes shows two entries for one stock — I believe this was originally a feature" | it never was; per-day deduplication was simply never built |

Five more faults turned up that only *rendering* could find, every one of them invisible in the source and passing a green suite: a volume readout that said "—" forever because the writer used one key and the reader another (a silent dictionary default, plausible and permanent); a clock rendering "09:0" because a font size set as a fraction of its band overran the column; the P&L popup driving straight **through** the player's own nametag, because the literal reading of "from the top of the player" is exactly where the tag lives; a booth background that was a hard step at 50% height rather than a gradient; and a menu page that never hid its sibling, so going back and forward drew two pages on top of each other.

**Then it got played, and four notes came back — and three were *again* a different fault from the symptom.** That's now the headline lesson of the whole pass: **a cosmetic complaint names where the player noticed something, almost never where it is.**

- **"Trader screens are upside down"** → the menu-board pods **hang** from above the counter, so the projection frame's idea of "up" was the prop's own up, pointing at the floor. It reads as a 180° rotation rather than a mirror because **an inverted prop flips both axes** — which is precisely why it slipped past every handedness assertion the suite already had, since those catch a flipped normal, which flips one axis alone.
- **And the first fix was wrong in an instructive way.** Squaring the content to *world* up sounds right and isn't: the pods are inverted **and tilted 15°**, so world-up produced level type inside a tilted bezel. The correct fix rotates the frame 180° in the prop's own plane. It's now pinned with a **165° probe prop**, because the two candidate fixes agree at exactly 180 and only disagree off-axis.
- **"Still fades to black before the daylight pops in"** → one line at each end. The environment leaves its ambient colour unset, which means black, so flipping the ambient *source* while the room is lit deletes all ambient in a single frame. The first pass had moved that switch to the **front** of the ramp, reasoning that a step at the end of a fade is worse — which relocated the cut to the bell instead of removing it. The rule: **a hard mode change belongs at the darkest moment of a ramp.**
- Measuring the rebuilt dawn found a fault **nobody reported**: brightness lerped on the clock had the sun at 79% while **58° below the horizon** — four of six seconds lighting the world from underneath. Direct light now rides the sun's own altitude.

One Godot detail worth passing on: **a literal `rotation.z` sweep holds a directional light completely still.** The Euler order is YXZ, direction is the negated basis z-axis, and a z-rotation never touches it. The turn has to be applied on the outside.

## Where things stand

Suite **1335/1335**, MP host 33/33 and client 14/14, both scenes booting clean. Both original tournament blockers are closed with about two weeks to spare.

What's left is short and mostly not code: a dated build handout, a 24-peer load test that has never been done, verifying the run log's gameplay lines in a real exported build rather than in the editor — and playing it. Everything from the polish pass onward is probe-and-screenshot verified only, and two rounds of notes on it found four real faults that no probe caught.
