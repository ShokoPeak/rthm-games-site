The longest gap between heartbeats so far, and the most ground covered. Twenty commits across seven sessions, and the test suite went 556 → 832. Almost all of it is economy work: two new venues for trading whole positions, the jackpot rebuilt as an insurance fund, a peer-to-peer comeback market, forced exits that finally cost something, and a trade-size rework that fixed a balance problem which had quietly been undermining the main game for months.

## EOD Calls: the ladder becomes the chart (Aug 3)

The last of the booth restyles from the previous cycle. EOD was the only booth with nothing to look at — a bet about where the price lands, showing one number and no history.

**The design in one line: the 17-rung band ladder *is* a price axis, so it became the y-axis of a full-day chart, and you place a call by clicking the strip you think the day closes in.**

- X spans the full session, never a trailing window, so the empty space right of the tape is the time you have left to be right.
- The price-to-y mapping is piecewise — middle bands map linearly, each tail covers a fixed span of band-widths before pinning — and it's continuous at both boundaries by construction. The hit test reads the same cached geometry as the plot, so a click can't arm a neighbouring band.
- **Your calls are gold, not the booth's brand green.** EOD's accent is nearly identical to the profit colour, which would make "my call" and "this won" the same colour on the one chart where that distinction is the entire payoff.
- The tape is one neutral colour: the y-axis here is bands, so per-bar up/down tint is noise and collides with settlement colouring.

### Balance was measured, not tuned

Matt's explicit call. Fourteen simulated days, two seeds, driven through the real game loop:

| | |
|---|---|
| closes in a middle band | **100%** |
| closes in a tail | **0%** |
| days closing DOWN | **13 / 14** |
| mean signed daily return | **−13.8%** |
| median absolute daily move | 11.2% (= 2.8 bands) |

**The booth is exploitable, and the cause isn't in the booth.** Always calling the same band below the open returns ~5.4× stake on average. That's not a payout-tuning problem — it's the market's systematic downward drift showing through a ladder laid out symmetrically about the open price. Fix the drift and the ladder is roughly fair; tune the ladder and you've hard-coded a market bug into the booth's geometry. Deferred, deliberately.

**One measurement gotcha worth the whole section:** burning bars fast by pumping the simulator *changes the answer*. Each call finishes at most one tick, so it looks like a clean fast-forward — but it collapses the ~60 intra-bar price updates into one, and order flow rides that signal. Same seed, same day: **+11.1% pumped versus −10.8% driven in real time.** A sign flip. Without that check the whole distribution above would have been confidently wrong.

## Two venues where whole books change hands (Aug 6)

Two ways to transfer an entire position between traders. They share one settlement path and deliberately **not** one pricing.

**The fire sale (forced).** A margin call now opens a ten-second sealed auction on the victim's book. Bids are named to the seller but blind between bidders; one open offer per bidder across the whole floor, so a cascade forces you to triage who you try to save; escrowed and binding until rejected. Rejection is the seller's only information channel and it costs them clock. A watch list shows who looked and passed — because "three people looked at me and passed" is a story, and "there was structurally nobody" is just silence.

**The upstairs market (voluntary).** Publicly quoted standing block offers at a price concession, for escaping the FIFO order queue with a large position. All or nothing — funding the whole book is the gate. The problem it solves gets worse every day by construction: the per-tick order budget grows linearly with the day while the goal curve compounds, so by day 20 a large position takes tens of bars to work through the queue, and the budget is shared by the whole lobby in strict FIFO. One whale's exit stalls everyone behind them.

**The pricing had to differ, and finding that out mid-build shaped the whole thing.** Forced pricing pays the seller a bid *on top of* a market exit while the buyer inherits the entry, so total equity moves by bid + buyer's unrealised. That's only safe because a margin-called book is worth less than nothing — the sum comes out negative and equity is destroyed. A voluntary seller **picks their moment**, so they'd pick a book that's up, where the same sum is positive, repeatable and collusion-friendly. A printer. Block trades therefore settle the way real ones do: one agreed price, both sides print at it. There's a regression assert that computes what the forced shape *would* mint, so a future "let's unify these" fails loudly.

Also in that pass: NPC sellers can answer their own auction (cover the hole and they accept, lowball early and they reject, past desperation they take what's there and may still bust), MP replication for both venues with a public broadcast plus a per-peer sealed half, and a fix for the MP harness returning a null game manager — which had been **silently no-opping the entire RPC surface under test**.

### The button that wasn't broken

First look at the block desk: *"the buttons don't have a good clickable area and it seems to lose focus with the price updates."*

The tab rebuilt itself every 0.25 s against a price that moves ~60× a bar, freeing every row. So the button under the cursor was destroyed and replaced mid-interaction — a press landed on a node that no longer existed by release, the pressed signal never fired, and hover flickered constantly. It reads to a player as "the button is broken", not as "the UI is rebuilding".

Fixed by splitting the refresh: a cheap signature of only what changes the *node tree* gates rebuilds, and everything volatile is re-applied in place on the same nodes. Setting text or disabled state never interrupts a click. The corollary is the part that generalises — **a control whose existence depends on a live value reintroduces the bug**, so buttons are now built unconditionally and greyed rather than swapped for a label. And the test asserts node *identity*, not count: "5 buttons before, 5 after" passes happily while every one is a different instance.

## The Relief Fund: the exchange insures itself (Aug 7)

The old jackpot became a **mutual insurance fund covering the whole exchange**, where members hold units and *are* the underwriters. You own a slice of everyone's losses, and you pay for everyone's blow-ups.

**The best fact in the whole design: the levy already existed.** Every trade on the floor already paid exactly 1%, charged out of the close, never itemised, from every agent including NPCs — and the money was being silently deleted. The entire "1% insurance levy, inclusive, invisible to the player" specification was already live. The only change needed was routing it somewhere instead of nowhere. No new cost to the player, no balance change.

The second-best fact: **the wider market already existed too, and already panics.** Ten hidden actors — retail, algorithmic, and contrarian institutional — have been writing net exposure into the simulation all along, and above a heat threshold they collapse their tranches to one-bar snaps and throw real orders into a book the heat system is simultaneously thinning. That's the stampede that gaps the price and margin-calls the overextended. The loop ran end to end already; nothing was metering it.

So the fund has four money paths: the floor levy in, an exchange levy metered off hidden-actor turnover in, floor defaults out, and **off-floor defaults out** — anonymous, riding the same heat signal, so a gap on the chart and a drop in NAV are one event seen from two sides. Those matter because if the fund only covered the room its risk would be fully observable. Off-floor defaults arrive with no visible cause on a bar where nothing happened in front of you. That's counterparty risk, and it's what holding an insurance fund actually feels like.

### It was a free lunch, and the haircut wasn't the problem

NAV compounded at **~88%/day**. The cause: premium income rides the goal curve, and the pot is the *cumulative sum* of that curve — and a geometric series sums to only ~2.13× its last term. So a risk-free instrument was paying almost exactly what hitting your target every day returns on your own stack.

The obvious dial can't fix it: scaling the income scales the pot too, so it cancels out of income ÷ pot and moves the yield by **zero**. It sets the fund's size, never its yield.

Fixed with **house retention** — a share of each premium is the exchange *buying units at the prevailing NAV*, not money skimmed off. The pot still grows at full rate and claim coverage is untouched, but every holder is diluted at the rate the economy compounds. Yield settles at ~10.3%/day, roughly 12× the ATM and about a ninth of trading. Claims still cut NAV at full force, so retention shrinks the *climb* and leaves the *falls* — the sawtooth had been drowned by the ramp.

It fixed the exit gradient for free, too. Redemption charges a **size-scaled haircut**: 25% of your wealth in costs 10.6% to leave, 50% costs 18.2%, 95% costs 27.8%, stable across a run. A small holder slips out for almost nothing; a whale who is all the way in cannot leave without cratering the very NAV they're fleeing. *"It's a trap, it's a gilded cage and if you fit all the way inside the fat cat might come and eat you."* Getting out quickly is instant by design — no lockups, no gating — but it is not cheap, and the size scaling **is** the mechanic.

**The sizing rule that keeps it working on day 1 and day 20:** meter the actors for *texture*, take the *magnitude* from the goal curve. Their exposure is denominated as a fraction of float; taken literally in dollars, one bar's levy on a large company would dwarf a day-1 player's entire stack. Read the actors for who is moving and whether this is a panic; get the dollars from the day.

## Pre-LAN multiplayer sweep (Aug 11)

Ahead of a mini-LAN session, everything got run: unit suite 728/728, MP harness 34/34 host and 18/18 client, and chart sync verified **byte-identical across all 481 candles after a rejoin**.

Two real bugs, both in work built since the last LAN test:

- The Relief Fund's unit ledger was sent with the **host's local agent ids** and applied verbatim on clients — so a client's own holding read zero, and it would have rendered the host's units as its own.
- **Market close isn't re-entrant**, and one sync RPC called it unguarded. A client could get pinned in the results beat forever and never advance the day.

**The harness itself was the bigger surprise: four of its seven failures were the test lying**, including one assertion that had been passing for the wrong reason. A green MP harness run is not evidence unless someone has looked at it.

One structural gap remains: the harness builds a *synthetic* scene, so it never loads the real floor and covers none of the fire sale, block desk, Relief Fund, EOD, prediction or binary work. A throwaway two-process probe against the real scene covered those once — that probe is the only thing that has ever caught a bug in this layer, and it's worth making permanent.

## Game modes, forced exits, and the comeback market (Aug 13)

### The modes are the menu

Game setup went from **nine rows to two**. The three multiplayer modes are now cards with a one-line pitch each — Co-op, Rumble, Trader Royale — chosen *before* hosting rather than configured after. Single player is Standard only, with no mode picker at all. Starting cash is locked, the bust-mode dropdown is gone, and the slot machine toggles went with it.

All of that is only safe because a single function now owns each mode's invariants, and every branch reproduces the deleted widgets' per-mode defaults exactly. One subtlety: the bust mode had to be *restored*, not just forced — with no dropdown left to correct it, playing Rumble once would otherwise latch its bust mode onto every later Standard run.

### Forced exits now cost something

The premise this feature started from was **wrong**, and it's worth recording: an old note claimed that closing the market exited every agent at one identical mid with no book walk. False — forced closes always took real, size-dependent slippage. A round was lost designing a penalty for a problem that didn't exist. But measuring properly turned up three defects that were worse, all from one line:

1. **Slippage was non-monotonic in size.** On one market $80k cost 0.914% and $320k cost **0.303%**. Selling four times more was three times cheaper. Every market had a trough where making your position *bigger* bought you a better fill.
2. **A flattened book was the cheapest place to trade.** With all layers dead, everything fell into a mid-priced pool — after a $50M dump, a $100k seller filled **45× better** than into a healthy book.
3. **So the closing bell had a free-rider ladder.** Five agents dumping $2M each: the first paid −0.472%, everyone after paid −0.068%. The order was registration order — an invisible, unearned, permanent seat advantage.

Net: the state was the exact *inverse* of a penalty. The bigger the crowd stuck at the bell, and the bigger your position, the cheaper your exit.

**The suite passed 825/825 before and after the fix.** Nothing covered any of it.

Now: forced exits eat a thinned book and print at their own volume-weighted average, the printed close is post-dump (so EOD Calls settles against it), and margin-call force-closes take the same penalty — which is what gives the fire sale and the block desk their value. A circuit breaker is mandatory, not optional: the goal curve compounds to ~$100M by day 20 while float is fixed, so a late stack can exceed a whole company. Unbounded, six day-20 holders printed **−100%, then NaN**.

### A bug can be load-bearing

Fixing that pricing defect immediately uncovered a recursion it had been holding shut. Order processing injects price impact, which emits a price update, which drives order processing — compounding about 20× per level.

It only fires when a tranche moves the price by more than 0.1%. A depleted book used to fill at roughly mid, so that was never true, and the loop never closed. Anchoring the overflow correctly made a depleted book fill about 1% out, and the whole path was exercised in anger for the first time: **3,547 stack overflows in a day, and an agent at −$127 trillion.**

**And nearly mis-called it.** The response to a $4.5tn claim was "that's not a plausible number" — and that was wrong, because this game compounds to $100M+ by day 20 and endless keeps going. What actually proved a runaway was **the same day's other claims: $59 and $70**, and the pot going $50,923 → $4.5tn. Comparison within the run, never a gut feeling about big numbers. In a game with an exponential goal curve, "that looks too big" is never the finding.

### The comeback market

The old revival was a genuine hole: one branch routed to a slot machine that had been dropped as off-brand, and the other silently handed busted players a full day's target in cash — a free lunch, in a game whose standing principle is that there aren't any.

What replaced it is **the fire sale inverted**. Instead of bidding to buy a dying position, a third player bids to **fund a dead trader's next day, on terms they set**. Three sticky ladders: the advance (1× to 100× the base rebuy), the profit cut (10–100%), and total repayment — 0%, which is an outright **gift**, then 100–500% with nothing in between, because repaying less than was lent isn't a discount, it's a worse-explained gift that moves money silently.

The mechanics that make it work:

- **The term is emergent — there is no day count.** The claim ends when it's satisfied: a good run clears it fast, a bad run carries it. The two dials are independent and that's the expressiveness — the cut sets the *pace*, the repayment sets the *cost*, so one rung can be brutal-but-brief and another cheap-but-dragging.
- **A bond can never be forced.** You can always decline, sit out the rest of the day, and come back tomorrow on the free standard rebuy. **So the bond sells exactly one thing: time.** Accepting revives you immediately, mid-day. That's the entire economic content of the offer, and it's what makes taking bad terms rational.
- Only positive profit is garnished, and the garnish runs *after* the day's P&L is computed — so it can never stop you hitting target. You did the work and you get the credit; what it costs is capital for tomorrow, against a target that grows by 88% a day.
- **A claim carries its creditor.** A player-funded comeback repays *that player*; only a house-funded one feeds the fund.
- NPCs both make and accept offers, priced by personality — the generous quote small and cheap and are the only source of gifts in single player; free-riders quote big money at punishing terms; chaos monkeys draw from the entire ladder. A reaction delay before quoting means offering on the frame someone busts doesn't read as a script, and rival lenders get a chance to be first.

**In single player the direction inverts: the player is the lender.** Your own bust is final — the only thing that saves you is an item bought beforehand. But a *bot's* window stays open, so you can back a defaulting trader and earn from their recovery. That's the fund's member fantasy made concrete: underwriting a person instead of a pot.

One thing that had to be fixed as a consequence: **the borrower must be able to see the loan.** Both legs are correctly invisible to P&L, but the scoreboard and the bankroll then disagree with nothing explaining the gap — the board says you hit target, then money silently leaves cash and tomorrow opens short. A decision made under pressure needs a visible consequence, or the terms you accepted never become real to you.

## Trade size, and a balance problem hiding in plain sight (Aug 17)

The biggest open design lever, finally picked up — and it turned into the most consequential balance finding in a while.

**Matt's playtest, day 2:** *"I was hardly making anything on the big swings. Meanwhile, I was able to blow past the goal and bonus with a handful of binary options."*

Measured on the quietest market in the game:

| route | per win | wins to clear the day |
|---|---|---|
| a good leveraged trade | $22.50 | **51** |
| a strong leveraged trade | $57.50 | 20 |
| **one binary option** | **$725** | **2** |

**~32× more efficient per correct call — and the cause was structural.** Every booth's top stake is a fraction of the day's goal, and trading's cap had been sitting *below* the casino's. The floor could not commit as much money as the booths could. On top of that the throttle was one-sided: booth ladders are goal-scaled and cash-blind, so a player *ahead* of par was capped in trading and free in the casino — exactly backwards from "the casino is side action".

It had been true for months without anyone noticing, and the symptom was the designer abandoning his own main game.

**The fix: trade size became a booth-style dollar ladder** — four rungs walked with the arrow keys, anchored to the run's *expected bankroll* rather than the day's goal. Day 1 offers $40 / $125 / $400 / $900; day 20 offers $4.5M / $15M / $45M / $100M.

Three rules carry it:

- **The top rung *is* the ceiling.** One number. When the ladder and the cap were separate, the HUD offered sizes the floor then trimmed.
- **Exactly one ceiling applies.** With a dollar ceiling set, the old percentage cap is ignored, not combined. Mining both is what made **14 of 24 risk rungs dead keypresses** — past day 1, only 10 produced a distinct trade size.
- **Anchored to the bankroll, not the goal.** A compounding run's *sum* is only 1.133× its last term, so bankroll ÷ goal starts at 1.667 and converges — a drift that walked the top rung from 75% of cash on day 1 to 110% by day 5, and past day 3 collapsed it to permanent all-in. Anchored to the bankroll it's flat by construction, and the cliff is gone. It also means falling behind shortens your ladder and getting ahead makes the top rung a smaller bite — no rule needed, it falls out of the anchor.

That ladder is now pinned by a test that asserts the ordering **against every booth**, on both sides, so a future retune of either can't quietly break it again.

### Binary options nerfed

*"We're gonna have to nerf the binary options a little."* Its top stake dropped from 75% of the day's goal to 45%. Notably the **size** was cut and not the payout — the coin flip still pays what it paid, so the break-even win rate is unchanged and it still feels fair. Cutting the payout would instead have raised the house edge, which also feeds Relief Fund income: a coupling worth remembering.

**And it had no concurrency limit, which made the stake cap meaningless.** Bets were appended without counting, and the stake is withdrawn at placement — so a player could put their **whole stack** into binary across N simultaneous bets while the floor held them to one rung. There's now a cap of three working bets, counted host-side before the withdraw, so settling frees a slot. It's a cap on exposure, never on volume.

**The generalisable lesson: a per-item cap is not a cap until something bounds the count.**

### Market conditions as a design lever

One more finding from that playtest, and it's a nice one. A large, quiet, deep-book market *disarmed* Matt's whole build — his read of the tape (nothing to read) and his margin stack (nothing to multiply) — and pushed him into the booths. He rated it as great gameplay, and wants it as a deliberate lever.

The price level is a **correlate, not the cause**: profit is notional × return × leverage, so the price cancels out entirely. What actually braked him was low volatility, a deep book he couldn't move himself, few sharp events, and the new dollar ceiling holding his size. Three brakes at once. The 16-market roster already runs that inverse axis — high price ↔ low volatility ↔ deep book ↔ big float — and difficulty is a U-shape, with very quiet and very wild both hard for opposite reasons. It just was never written down as a mechanism, and nothing in the game yet tells a player which kind of day they're walking into.

## Where things stand

Suite at **832/832**. Everything above is committed and pushed.

**Blocked on design rulings:** the comeback bond's remaining questions, and co-op's margin reservation — several traders each sizing a percentage of the *same* shared pot. Both shape the shared-bankroll rework, which is the last big piece of co-op and touches the whole order chain.

**One build piece left** on the comeback market: multiplayer replication, modelled on the block desk's public/private split.

**Known and unmeasured:** Relief Fund claim frequency from *floor* busts is still zero in every probe — every claim so far has been an off-floor default, which means the deeper holes the new forced-exit penalty creates have never been exercised in a measured session. The suspicion is the harness rather than the design: those runs managed 3–5 frames per bar against a real ~60, which thins order flow about 15× and would suppress exactly the margin calls being looked for. That one needs a played session, not another probe.

Two booth restyles remain, the feedback platform still owes its final phase, and the exported build has still never been run host-and-client as a real `.exe`. Alpha 2 in September is the horizon.
