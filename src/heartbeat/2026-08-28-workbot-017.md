A hard date landed this week: a **50-person LAN tournament at the end of September**, which turns a rolling roadmap into a critical path. The work that followed was the booth identity programme — four of the five casino booths rebuilt with their own layouts — plus a new item and two standing rules about what player-facing copy is allowed to say.

## The complaint, and why the cause was the inverse

*"All of the different TCs are using very similar theming with colour, text etc and not reflecting the available assets or colour schemes. There's not enough personality to each item."*

Auditing it turned up the opposite of what that describes. **The chrome had diverged while the identity was missing** — backwards from how a design system is supposed to fail:

- **Four of five brand typefaces ship and were never used.** They had exactly one consumer between them: the scrolling name band on the physical post. Every booth had a brand out on the floor; none of them had one on its page.
- **Brand colour had three sources of truth and they disagreed** — the in-world post, the browser chrome, and a panel-local constant. One booth was one gold on its post and a different gold on its own page.
- **The neutral ink ramp was copy-pasted four times with drift.** That is *why* they looked alike (same three greys) while also being inconsistent.
- Structure — the thing that should have been identical — was **five different corner radii: 0, 4, 6, 8 and 11**.
- **The shared booth theme bakes the binary desk's gold into its primary buttons and borders**, so the pink page, the cyan page, the green page and the red page had all been rendering gold buttons. Probably the single largest cause.
- And the neutrals are **cool blue-grey under warm accents**, so the accent is the only warm thing on the page. It reads as applied rather than intrinsic.

**The ruling: bespoke layout per booth**, piloted one at a time. The cheaper option — shared chrome plus brand tokens — was recommended and rejected with its cost stated. Personality has to come from the **layout**, not only from colour and type.

## The four passes

**Binary desk** — bands rather than a rail, a bisected ticket, a full-width tape, three physical working slots. It earned bands because **its data is bounded**: a hard cap of three working bets means nothing ever scrolls, and a rail exists to hold a growing list. Dead weight, so it went.

**Predator** — the inverse constraint. Its quote board *grows*, so it keeps its rail. One column grouped by trigger-versus-deadline, the brand folded into the title row as a masthead, square tinted price cells, a plum ramp.

**Launch Pad** — the one that produced the general rules, after a note that it was *"a little too cartoony... I want this to look sharp, like an actual trading website."* The four levers, in the order they actually mattered:

1. **Emoji are the loudest single tell.** A skull on the banner, a rocket on the primary button, and a rocket/cross/flag opening every ledger row. Removing them changed the read more than anything else in the pass.
2. **Corner radius.** The shared button style carries a radius of 8 — right for the in-world HUD, wrong for a terminal panel. A local square-cornered plate instead.
3. **Columns, not sentences.** A ledger row reading `$COIN stake $175.00 -> $224.33 (+$49.33)` gives the eye nothing to scan down. Three fixed columns — tag, subject, right-aligned P&L — is what a blotter looks like.
4. **Hue count.** A neutral ramp for everything structural; colour reserved for values that mean something.

And the bigger lesson, from *"the bar at the bottom with the red trajectory is confusing as nothing happens in it"*: the rug-risk ramp had its own pane, and the ramp is **deterministic** — identical on every run of every coin. A pane devoted to a static curve animates never, and sitting next to a live one it reads as a second data series that is broken. **Ask of any sub-plot: does this change between runs?** If not it isn't a series — make it the chart's ground, a rule, or a number in a tile. Folding it in also freed the whole height for the price, which is what a trader is actually looking at.

The Launch Pad's house edge was also rebuilt to come from **chance rather than a rake**, and its stake scalar dropped from 0.40 to 0.15 off the measured table.

Two findings from its layout worth keeping. Its action rail went on the **left**, because the chart's x-axis is time and the space to the right is the runway you have left — but **moving a rail is not a neutral operation**. Predator's rail pins settled rows to the bottom with an expanding gap; on the right that's the page's trailing edge, and on the left the same gap is a 300px hole in the first column your eye lands on, which reads as a panel that failed to load. *Same pixels, opposite reading.* Only rendering it showed that.

And it had been rendering **the binary desk's gold as its own accent in three places** — including the coin name, the most identity-carrying string on the page — through a constant named `COL_GOLD`. Named for a colour rather than for a booth, so a reader could not see it was foreign.

**EOD Calls** — the page *is* the ladder, full width, with a thin action strip underneath; the book moved out of the rail and onto the bands as `stake -> pays`. It surfaced two rules:

- **When a booth's accent collides with a semantic colour, both house levers vanish.** EOD's accent is nearly the profit colour, and on that page green already means "this band won" — so the ramp couldn't go toward the brand. Gold was gone too, already spoken for as "yours" *precisely because* of that collision. The answer was a desaturated emerald under two opposing constraints, with **ground direction** doing the separating instead.
- **Two booths must not claim the same axis in the same direction.** The Launch Pad had already claimed "darkest page of the five", so EOD went lifted.

## The trap that shipped a booth you couldn't use

Every pass in this programme **reparents authored nodes** rather than rebuilding them, so existing references, signals and authored connections survive. On EOD that broke the booth outright:

**`add_child` refuses a node that already has a parent — it does not reparent.** One bare call left CANCEL and PLACE CALL sitting in the rail that had just been hidden, so **no call could be placed at all**. The commit path was untouched and worked perfectly when invoked directly; the buttons were simply unreachable. A restructure that hides a container also has to *enumerate* its children rather than moving the ones you remember — the status label was left behind too, so every refusal ("Pick a stake first", "Not enough cash") wrote into an invisible label.

**The layout probe passed 11/11 while this was broken.** It asserted page rects, the rail being gone, the strip existing — never whether the thing you press is reachable. The fix is one assertion: **`is_visible_in_tree()` on the primary action**. That single property distinguishes "moved" from "orphaned in a hidden branch", and neither a rect check nor a screenshot of the unarmed state can see it.

## A new item, and two rules about copy

**UNCAPPED LOSSES** — a shop item that re-bases your maximum trade size on your total trading cash, so winnings buy bigger trades. Its downside is deliberately not in the description: the ladder's *bottom* rung rises with the top, so the cheap probing trade disappears. Which produced a standing rule:

> **"Players need to be aware of the mechanic more than the consequences."**

State the rule the player is being asked to operate; let the fallout be discovered. The surprise *is* the lesson — you buy the item for the reach, then reach for a small trade and find it gone, and that's learned once and never forgotten. A clause in a shop description spends that moment for nothing. (The exception: anything that can silently take money or end a run with no visible cause. Discovery means the player can work out what happened; obscurity means they can't.)

The second rule is narrower and non-negotiable: **no real brand names in player-facing copy.** A rug-pull flavour line read `dev wallet emptied · telegram deleted · stake gone`. A real company's name inside a scam narrative is a storefront liability and the joke never depended on it — `socials deleted` is funnier for being generic. It applies to booth flavour, ticker headlines, chat barks, item descriptions and coin names alike; invented parody words are fine.

**Next:** the fifth booth, and the balance work the tournament's validity depends on — if the casino is easily profitable, the winner is whoever exploited the booths hardest rather than whoever traded well.
